\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Optimisation can be thought of as a tool-selection problem -- with
different optimisation paradigms being the tools in your toolbox. When
dealing with a certain optimisation problem, the properties of the
problem will inform which tools would perform best on it. Each tool
would have their unique set of tradeoffs -- you \emph{could} use a
sledgehammer to nail together a wooden frame, and using a ball-peen
hammer to take down a wall is certainly\ldots{} possible, though it
clearly would be more exhausting to tackle each of those problems in
such a way.

When discussing optimisation algorithms (henceforth, ``optimisers''),
`No Free Lunch' (NFL) theorems inform us that finding an optimiser that
is \emph{suited} to a certain problem is how we realise the benefits of
different optimisers: ``any elevated performance over one class of
problems is offset by performance over another class''
(\citeproc{ref-wolpert1997}{Wolpert and Macready 1997}). Though, how
would a poorly designed tool compare to a well-made one? How could such
a thing fit into our broader NFL environment? Clearly a paper teaspoon
would not be part of our greater toolbox of optimisers. We will explore
how to evaluate inefficiencies or redundancy despite an understanding of
NFL theorems.

Regarding optimisers, it is typical that those which make use of
gradients perform better than those which use the problem's function
value directly -- with methods making use of hessian information
outperforming even those. Though it is important to note the limitations
that both hessian and gradient methods (we can call these `derivative'
methods) do depend on being able to calculate or approximate gradients
or hessians for the optimisation problem at hand. It can also be noted
that direct optimisers can have advantages over derivative methods. For
optimisation problems where hessians and gradients can be found, there
exists the possibility of them being harmful to an optimiser. These
could be unstable to calculate, deceptive/misleading, or otherwise
non-informative to an optimiser. Where this is not the case though, use
of algorithms which can take advantage of gradients or hessians would be
\emph{expected} to be superior.

For those cases where usage of either gradients or hessians are
impossible or otherwise infeasible, direct methods are available. While
this flexibility in which problems this type of optimiser can be applied
to is beneficial, direct methods losing the information available from
gradients or hessians would either have no effect or even a detrimental
effect on an algorithm's performance in optimisation. It would not be
useful to use such a method when ones better suited to a problem exist.
For this reason direct methods like the genetic algorithm are most
informatively compared with fellow direct methods. Therefore, this paper
will primarily discuss the difference between genetic algorithms and
their closest competitors: derivative-free optimisers, also known as
black-box (or direct) methods.

The genetic algorithm (GA) is a pioneering algorithm in the space of
nature-inspired optimisers---algorithms which look at processes in our
natural world, and hope to capture what these natural processes can
accomplish in the language of mathematics. It evokes incredibly powerful
imagery, with Darwin's theory of natural selection, as well as our
broader understanding of genetics. In fact, at first glance, the typical
binary-coded genetic algorithm could easily be used as a standin for
actual genetic code! If we pair off each `digit' in our encoding, we
could have a quaternary system that could represent the C, G, A, and T
nucleobases of DNA directly. While quite exciting, the comparisons do
end soon though, since genetic encodings do not typically go as far to
replicate the double stranded nature of DNA, nor the fact that
nucleobases must bind to each other in specific pairings (C \& G, and A
\& T). It could be argued that GAs are instead replicating genetic
information being stored in RNA rather than DNA. While it is theorised
that this method of storing life's genetic information in RNA is
possible and could perhaps have been the origins of life, no direct
evidence of this remains, and the natural world as we know it does not
itself function in such a manner.

\section{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

The early descriptions of GAs as popularised by Holland
(\citeproc{ref-holland1975}{1975}) had parameters represented in bits,
i.e., binary-coded GAs.

The development of the real-coded genetic algorithm (RCGA) came as a
result of challenges that came with binary-coded genetic algorithms.

RCGAs typically require a set of bounds -- upper and lower bounds which
we sample within (etcetc) -- over which to optimise (they are search
algorithms). If we use an random search (RS) method, we can do much the
same.

Eventually came GA's application to multiobjective optimisation, since
issues with constraint satisfaction had been remedied, and GAs showed a
promising ability to take to high-dimensional problems.

By the end of the 20th century, GA had already become viewed as a
powerful tool for both discrete and continuous optimisation.

Currently much focus has been placed on `hybrid' algorithms. Algorithms
which do not solely use GA: adding other algorithms to capture the
global exploration benefits GA might offer, but with local search
capabilities of other algorithms (\citeproc{ref-chaudhary2026}{Chaudhary
et al. 2026}).

The forefront of RCGA development has been on finding and designing
crossover \& mutation methods which lend themselves to performing well
with the continuous nature of the parameters.

How much can we change an algorithm before it ceases to be what it
promised to be? The changes in these algorithms could now place them in
a significantly different sphere than originally.

We will look into some of the simpler crossover and mutation methods
later in this paper. Comparison to the newer adaptive methods of
crossover and mutation will show that GAs have become something entirely
different to what the metaphors had once started with.

The NFL theorems might be taken to tell us that every algorithm is
valuable, since any dip in performance in any set of problems, is
countered by the performance an algorithm has in the complement of that
set. While we might argue that the usefulness of all problems in the
universal set is not equal -- that their applicability to real world
problems is not uniformly weighted -- even if we accept this without
scrutiny, we still have avenues to argue that indeed not all algorithms
are equally valuable.

The NFL theorems necessitate that an algorithm does not return to
previous points. This might seem like it immediately disqualifies most
methods -- or is practically infeasible -- but any algorithm can be made
to meet this requirement. Simply by storing the inputs and outputs
(fitness value) to every objective function (fitness function) call, we
meet the requirements to apply NFL. If before we call the objective
function, we search if that combination of inputs has already been
evaluated, and only then call the objective function if it has not --
using the fitness value we previously stored in the case of a repeated
call. This can ensure that we never evaluate the objective function at
the same point more than once. Now this does add overhead, which may
become significant, especially where objective function calls are cheap.
But where objective function calls become complex and expensive, it is
often worth the memory and processor usage.

It should be easy to imagine an algorithm that could be considered
especially ineffective. Though it is important to note that NFL theorems
also seem to require that an algorithm is \emph{capable} of visiting all
points, so our simplest ideas (some algorithm that repeatedly searches a
single point or small set of points) for something ineffective will not
actually fit. We could find a fitting candidate for our ineffective
algorithm if we imagine an algorithm that often repeats the points it
has previously visited: perhaps as the amount of points it has visited
increases, it finds itself more and more likely to repeat points instead
of finding a new point. Perhaps it visits each order combination of
previously visited points before searching a new point -- thus having
\(O(n!)\) complexity! We can compare this to a related algorithm that
skips the repeated evaluations and instead directly searches the exact
same new points without these artificial inefficiencies. These two
algorithms would now, having applied the aforementioned method to make
any algorithm NFL theorem compatible, the exact same signature. The
precisely same order of function evaluations. Yet clearly, these
algorithms have nowhere near the same value, even if viewed as the same
in NFL theorems. Of course, this is not a fair argument against NFL. But
with this extreme argument, it can be shown that there definitively is
the ability for some algorithms to be better or worse to apply to the
problem of optimisation. Certainly, there exists No Free Lunch, but
there certainly \emph{can} exist an overpriced lunch.

We now have an avenue of criticism for our algorithms: whether the
overhead of our optimisation method renders it unproductive.

Considering the scepticism into nature-inspired methods in the last
decade, it is only natural to scrutinise one of the foremost pillars in
nature-inspired methods with the same inspecting light that newer
methods have been cast in. While the influx of nature-inspired methods
has been cause for concern for the entire field of metaheuristics
(\citeproc{ref-aranha2021}{Aranha et al. 2021}), the RCGA has been left
mostly unscathed. Let us explore how much of that is due to their
inherent performance, and how much may be due to their proximity to the
performance GAs more broadly.

\section{Design}\label{design}

\subsection{Update procedures}\label{update-procedures}

Where noted, a \emph{strictly improving update procedure} will be used
alongside the updating equations, defined as:
\[\B x_{k+1} = \begin{cases}\B v_{k+1}&\text{if} \quad \op{f}{\B v_{k+1}}< \op{f}{\B x_k},\\\B x_k & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]
Where \(\B x_k\) is the best performing point at iteration \(k\), and
\(\B v_{k+1}\) is the candidate point for the next iteration, \(k+1\).
This candidate point is generated by the \textbf{updating equation}.

Alternatively, the update procedure could be a simpler, \emph{direct
updating procedure}, where every updated point, \(\B v_{k+1}\), is taken
as the next iteration (thus the algorithm remains the same, but every
\(\B x_k\) in the above formula is replaced with \(\B v_k\)). No
comparison with previous points is done. This would likely fit the
deterministic algorithms better. The best solution at point \(k\),
\(\B x_k\), is stored separately as a vector -- never accessed by the
algorithm.

\subsection{Updating equations}\label{updating-equations}

At the core of this exploration of genetic algorithms and other methods
for optimisation, are the \emph{updating equations}. These define how
each optimisation paradigm attempts to iteratively improve a candidate
solution, \(\B v\).

\subsubsection{Hessian methods}\label{hessian-methods}

Methods which utilise exact or approximate hessians.

\paragraph{Newton-Raphson}\label{newton-raphson}

Calculates the hessian.
\[\B x_{k+1} = \B v_k -\frac{f'\left(\B v_k\right)}{f''\left(\B v_k\right)}\]

\subsubsection{Gradient methods}\label{gradient-methods}

Methods which utlise exact or approximate gradients.

\paragraph{Gradient descent}\label{gradient-descent}

\[\B v_{k+1} = \B v_k -\nu \nabla f\left( \B v_k \right),\] where
\(\nu\) is the learning rate.

\subsubsection{Direct search methods}\label{direct-search-methods}

Also known as pattern search or black-box methods. These algorithms do
not make use of gradient nor of hessian information. This makes them far
easier to apply to any context.

\paragraph{Random search (RS)}\label{random-search-rs}

The specific version of the random search algorithm is informed by the
descriptions given in Zabinsky (\citeproc{ref-zabinsky2011}{2011}).
Algorithms are described by being `global' or `local'.

\textbf{Pure Random Search (PRS)} repeatedly performs a global search
with a simply improving update procedure.

The updating equation is defined as, \[\B v_{k+1} = \B u.\]

It is notable that neither of the terms \(\B x_k\) nor \(\B v_k\) -- the
best or candidate points found by or for iteration \(k\) -- are present
in this formula.

Each generated point is \emph{independent} of any previous point. The
major benefit of this is PRS is `embarrassingly parallelisable'. This is
the only optimisation algorithm that will have this property in this
paper.

We also test an additional variant of RS, where a global search is
performed solely for the starting point, \(\B x_0\), or skipped entirely
in favour of a user-defined starting point. Subsequent points are
generated locally from the hypersphere (referred to as the
`\(\left(N-1\right)\)-sphere', or `surface of the \(N\)-ball') around
\(\B x_k\).

This is Rastrigin's \textbf{Fixed Step Size Random Search
(FSSRS)}(\citeproc{ref-rastrigin1963}{Rastrigin 1963}). Rastrigin is
also credited with coining the term `random search', though the name
FSSRS is a term given by others to distinguish this original form of RS
from other optimisers.

An efficient way to sample uniformly from the surface of an \(N\)-ball
is given by Marsaglia (\citeproc{ref-marsaglia1972}{1972}).

\[\B v_{k+1} = \B x_k +h \B u.\]

Here, \(\B u\) is a normalised \(N\)-dimensional vector sample from the
standard normal distribution such that,

\(\B u = \frac1{\lVert \B z \rVert}\B z\) and
\(\B z \sim \mathcal{N}_N\left(0,1\right),\) and where \(h\) is a step
size hyperparameter.

\section{Genetic algorithms: the GA
package}\label{genetic-algorithms-the-ga-package}

Genetic algorithms are a subset of evolutionary algorithms, typified by
the use of three genetic operators: two-parent \textbf{crossover},
fitness-based \textbf{selection}, and random \textbf{mutation}.

\emph{Real-coded} (in reference to \(\mathbb R\), the real number set)
genetic algorithms are used in this paper for greater applicability to
arbitrary problems and models -- despite genetic processes occurring
with a discrete set of genes (ACGT) in nature.

Notation does shift here, due to the genetic algorithm necessarily
requiring multiple candidates for each iteration. Our previous notation
referencing a single candidate per iteration is thus no longer feasible.

We are implementing genetic algorithms using the \texttt{GA} package, as
described in Scrucca (\citeproc{ref-scrucca2013}{2013}).

A brief explanation of the updating procedure follows.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Fitness
evaluation}}{Fitness evaluation}}\label{fitness-evaluation}

Fitness for each of the candidate solutions (`genotypes'),
\(\theta_i^{\left(k\right)}\) is calculated, where \(i\) is an arbitrary
index of the candidate solution in the generation \(k\). This is
typically the objective function of interest, \(\op f{\cdot}\).

\subsection{Genetic Operators}\label{genetic-operators}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Selection}}{Selection}}\label{selection}

A subset of candidate solutions from the current generation's population
becomes selected as a separate population known as the `reproducing
population'. Solutions are selected (with replacement) through a
weighted selection process, based typically proportionally on the
fitness of each genotype, \(\op{f\!}{\theta_i^{\left(k\right)}}\), with
probability \(p_i^{\left(k\right)}\).

An elitist strategy can be used, which means that even if the best
performers are unluckily not selected, an amount of them are still are
pushed through to the next population. The default method for
real-valued parameters is \texttt{gareal\_lsSelection}: proportional
selection with fitness linear scaling.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Selection with linear scaling: \texttt{gareal\_lsSelection()}]
Let $\B f$ contain the fitness values of each member in the current population.\\
\\
To ensure all fitness values are positive:
$$
\B f \leftarrow \begin{cases}
\B f- \op{min}{\B f} & \text{if } \op{min}{\B f} < 0,\\
\B f & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$
Then, $\B f$ is scaled as follows.\\
\\
Let the mid-range, $M=\frac{\op{min}{\B f} +\op{max}{\B f}}2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta &=
\begin{cases}
\op{max}{\B f} - \bar f 
& \text{if } \bar f > M,
\\
\bar f -\op{min}{\B f} 
& \text{otherwise,}
\\
\end{cases}
\\
a&= 
\frac{\bar f}{\delta} \\
b&= 
\frac{\bar f}{\delta}\left(\delta - \bar f\right) = \bar f(1-\frac{\bar f}{\delta})
\end{aligned}
$$
$\B f' = a\B f + b$.\\
\\
The probabilities for sampling with replacement are calculated from the scaled fitness values, $\B f'$, where:
$$ p_i = \frac{f'_i}{\sum_j^Nf'_j}.$$

\end{tcolorbox}

Note that this is not the exact algorithm as contained within Scrucca's
GA package. I have taken the liberty to simplify and rearrange terms for
clarity of analysis.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Crossover}}{Crossover}}\label{crossover}

The core of a genetic algorithm, the crossover operation, brings a means
to generate `child' (next generation) solutions through use of the
`genetic information' (parameter values) from either of the `parent'
(current generation) solutions. Alongside mutation, crossover applied on
the reproducing population creates the new, next generation population.
The default method for real-valued parameters is
\texttt{gareal\_laCrossover}: local arithmetic crossover. These
functions take two `parent' solutions and generate two `child'
solutions.

Before any crossover algorithm is applied, a preprocessing step is done
to pair solutions into `mating' pairs.

Where \(N\) is the population size, and \(n_p\) is the number of parents
selected for crossover. \[
\begin{aligned}
n_p &= 2\lfloor \frac{N}2\rfloor ,
\\
\B\theta^k_1, \dots, \B\theta^k_{n_p}  &\in_R \Theta^k,
\end{aligned}
\] such that each candidate solution \(\B\theta_i\) is selected
uniformly randomly without replacement from \(\Theta^k\), and organised
into pairs for the crossover algorithm.

After the selection phase, solutions from the population are randomly
split into pairs. These will serve as the parents from which a pair of
child solutions will be calculated.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Local arithmetic crossover: \texttt{gareal\_laCrossover()}]
Where $\B a$ is a vector of standard uniform deviates.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\B a &\sim \mathcal U\!\left(0,1\right)\\
\B\theta_1^{k+1} &= \B a \circ \B\theta_1^k +\left(1-\B a\right)\circ\B\theta_2^k \\
\B\theta_2^{k+1} &= \B a \circ \B\theta_2^k +\left(1-\B a\right)\circ\B\theta_1^k
\end{aligned}
$$
A pair of child solutions, $\B\theta_1^{k+1}$, $\B\theta_2^{k+1}$, are calculated as weighted mean of the parents' parameter values. For each pair of parent solutions, $\B\theta^k_1$, $\B\theta^k_2$, the weighting, $\B a$, is a vector of size $p$ of random deviates from a standard uniform distribution. Each child is calculated as one of the combinations of parent solutions.\\

This process is performed for each pair of parent candidate solutions.
\end{tcolorbox}

This is a simple crossover paradigm. While it is \emph{the default}
method applied to real-coded parameters, we do not want to risk
strawmanning GAs in this analysis. Since crossover sits at the core of a
GA's ability to explore a parameter space, if a method of crossover is
not able to do so well, it will easily kneecap the entire algorithm as a
whole. To account for this, we shall also take a look at the other
real-coded crossover methods found in the GA package. While not a
comprehensive look at everything GAs have to offer, we are able to look
at the convenient tools most analysts would be reaching for. This feels
more appropriate to ensure meaningful results and discussion can come
from this analysis.

Within the GA package, we see that the crossover methods are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[{[default]}] \verb|gareal_laCrossover|: local arithmetic crossover,
  \item[--] \verb|gareal_waCrossover|: whole arithmetic crossover,
  \item[--] \verb|gareal_blxCrossover|: blend crossover,
  \item[--] \verb|gareal_laplaceCrossover|: laplace crossover,
  \item[{[missing]}]\verb|gareal_spCrossover|: single-point crossover,
  \item[{[missing]}]\verb|gareal_uCrossover|: uniform crossover.
\end{itemize}

*There are two additional methods mentioned in Scrucca
(\citeproc{ref-scrucca2013}{2013}) which appear not to be present in the
codebase.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Whole arithmetic crossover: \texttt{gareal\_waCrossover()}]
Similar to local arithmetic crossover, but where $a$ is a shared single value for all parameters rather than a vector of standard uniform values.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &\sim \mathcal U\!\left(0,1\right)\\
\B\theta_1^{k+1} &= a \B\theta_1^k +\left(1-a\right)\B\theta_2^k \\
\B\theta_2^{k+1} &= a \B\theta_2^k +\left(1-a\right)\B\theta_1^k
\end{aligned}
$$
A pair of child solutions, $\B\theta_1^{k+1}$, $\B\theta_2^{k+1}$, are calculated as weighted mean of the parents' parameter values. For each pair of parent solutions, $\B\theta^k_1$, $\B\theta^k_2$, the weighting is random value, $a$, from a standard uniform distribution. Each child is calculated as one of the combinations of parent solutions.\\

This process is performed for each pair of parent candidate solutions.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Blend crossover: \texttt{gareal\_blxCrossover()}]
With a blend parameter $a$ that defaults to $0.5$.
\\

For a pair of parent candidate solutions, $\B\theta_1$ and $\B\theta_2$, each with $p$ parameters, and for each pair of corresponding parameters, $\theta_{1j}$ and $\theta_{2j}$, within those candidate solutions, the following is applied.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1 &= \op\min{\theta_{1j}, \theta_{2j}}, \\
x_2 &= \op\max{\theta_{1j}, \theta_{2j}}, \\
\\
b_j &= x_1 - a\left(x_2-x_1\right), \\
c_j &= x_2 + a\left(x_2-x_1\right), \\
\\
b_j &\leftarrow 
\begin{cases}
b_j &\text{if } b_j>\text{min}_j,\\
\text{min}_j &\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}\\
c_j &\leftarrow 
\begin{cases}
c_j &\text{if } c_j<\text{max}_j,\\
\text{max}_j &\text{otherwise},
\end{cases} \\
\\
u_1,u_2 &\sim \mathcal U \!\left(b_j,c_j\right),\\
\theta_{1j} &\leftarrow u_1,\\
\theta_{2j} &\leftarrow u_2,\\
\end{aligned}
$$
for all $j$ in $\left\{1, \dots, p\right\}$.\\

This process is performed for each pair of parent candidate solutions.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Laplace crossover: \texttt{gareal\_laplaceCrossover()}]
Scrucca references @deep2009 for the recommendation of $a=0$ and $b=0.15$ for the location and scale parameters to be used with the Laplace distribution for the case of real-coded genetic algorithms.

These are the default values for this method, though overwritable when called.

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_j,u_j &\sim \op{\mathcal U}{0,1},\\
\beta_j &= \begin{cases}
a+b\log\left(u_j\right) & \text{if } r_j > 0.5, \\
a-b\log\left(u_j\right) & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}\\
\\
s_j &= \beta_j \lvert \theta_{1j} - \theta_{2j} \rvert, \\
\\
t_{1j} &= \theta_{1j} + s_j,\\
t_{2j} &= \theta_{2j} + s_j, \\
\\
\theta_{1j} &\leftarrow 
\begin{cases}
\min_j & \text{if } t_{1j} \leq \min_j,\\
t_{1j} & \text{if } \min_j < t_{1j} < \max_j,\\
\max_j & \text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}\\
\theta_{2j} &\leftarrow
\begin{cases}
\min_j & \text{if } t_{2j} \leq \min_j,\\
t_{2j} & \text{if } \min_j < t_{2j} < \max_j,\\
\max_j & \text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
for all $j$ in $\left\{1, \dots, p\right\}$.\\

This process is performed for each pair of parent candidate solutions.
\end{tcolorbox}

Notably, Laplace crossover is the only method which does not have the
issue of its child parameter value being constrained to the range given
by its parent values.

It is clear that nearly regardless of the method chosen for crossover,
the outcome is quite similar. The parameter value of the child will be
limited in scope to the values given by the parents. This necessarily
means that diversity in the solution space will gradually dwindle as the
algorithm progresses. This lack of diversity will ensure the algorithm
is less and further less able to better the fitness value as the
population homogenises, due to not being able to effectively explore the
solution space.

To combat this -- other than avoiding the use of crossover methods with
are not the Laplace method provided -- we can allow for mutation to help
disturb our settling into any part of the solution space, and allow us
to increase diversity.

Essentially regularisation! We are turning the mutation operators into
random search!

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Mutation}}{Mutation}}\label{mutation}

The randomly shifting of the values of `genes' (parameters) within
genotypes. The probability of mutation can be set by the user. Alongside
crossover, mutation applied on the reproducing population creates the
new, next generation population. By default, \texttt{gareal\_raMutation}
is used, which is uniform random mutation.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Uniform random mutation: \texttt{gareal\_raMutation()}]
Generates a uniform random deviate for one randomly selected parameter value, constrained on the bounds for that specific parameter.\\
\\
The new mutated value is completely unaffected by whatever value the original parameter held.

$$\theta_j \in_R \B \theta$$
$$x\sim \mathcal U \! \left( \text{min}_j, \text{max}_j \right)$$
$$\B \theta _j \leftarrow x$$
\end{tcolorbox}

Crossover and mutation operations intend to increase diversity in a
population, allowing us to explore more of the sample space
(exploration), while selection operations decrease diversity in favour
of increased fitness (exploitation).

Figure~\ref{fig-pure-rs} showcases just how lucky PRS can really be. The

best performing solution at iteration 45, or 86, or 93. Paying close
attention, the best candidate solution was found on approximately the
18th iteration of the algorithm. While that sounds very optimistic, it
also means the algorithm had run for approximately 82 iterations with no
improvement. The fact that it got so lucky on the 18th iteration would
not be something you could rely on over multiple runs, or multiple
different problems. This issue only gets worse as a problem's dimensions
increase. While PRS does have a uniform probability to reach any point
in the search space, and does not have the issue of getting `stuck' in
local optima, there is no guarantee of improvement as the algorithm
runs. Improvements in this algorithm are proportional to the performance
of the best point currently found: Let the comprehensive set of possible
solutions in the search space be \(U\). We can collect all possible
solutions which perform better than the best solution found by the
optimiser at iteration \(k\), \(x_k\), into a set, \(S\). and \(S\)
takes up 5\% of \(U\) then there is only a probability of 5\% that the
candidate solution \(v_{k+1}\) has better performance than \(x_k\).

This can help us identify another place where performance of PRS might
suffer or improve. Even certain properties of PRS. The distribution of
performance for the possible solutions in a specific problem changes, so
too does the effectiveness of optimisers like PRS. Since PRS is
uniformly able to select any point in the solution space, we
theoretically could `rank' all possible points by their performance.
This would let us obtain for any problem a curve of the performance
values by percentile. And knowing how improvement is proportional to the
(complement of the) performance percentile of a problem, we could take
an expected value or confidence interval for what percentile we could
expect to receive after \(k\) iterations.

\subsection{Measuring Performance}\label{measuring-performance}

An interesting issue with drawing comparisons between GAs and
optimisation procedures is the issue of fairly assessing the amount of
function calls. We could simply compare the iteration number like we
have with the other optimisation procedures. We would have to note how
some procedures require more overhead for each function call, but until
now, these overheads above the function call were not too excessive. Now
with GAs, we have an entire population being assessed with each
iteration. The GA package (Scrucca 2017) uses a default population size
of 50, and throws a warning if the population size is less than 10.

A solution to this is taking the product of the iteration number and
population size to get a value of function calls completed by the
process. This does seem to be the fairest assessment method. When
attempting this, we might use the iteration limit of 100 that we have
currently been using, and divide that by the population size being used.

Whoops. Using the default value, we've reached our maximum amount of
function calls in two population iterations.

Clearly, we have not given GAs a fair chance to flex their muscles.
There has barely been enough time for any of the `learning' that GAs are
capable of to come through in a single step.

So perhaps we try a more balanced value: 10 iterations of population
size 10.

Ah phooey. We now have the issue of rarer events like mutation
\(\left(p=0.1\right)\) only occurring once on average per population
step. Additionally, 10 population steps is still squeezing the algorithm
quite a bit. This seems hardly fair. Random search will have trouble
making any meaningful progress towards a solution if it was given 10
iterations. Even gradient methods would have the issue of having
suboptimal results with 10 iterations. Therefore, this is clearly not an
issue that lands solely with direct search methods.

Perhaps we could consider \emph{unique} function calls. This may be a
better fit. GAs do have a tendency to repeatedly check the same values.
Well-performing genes will survive to the next generation, to be tested
again. Crossover does complicate this, but it should be clear that in
principle, the probability of collisions is increased through this form
of performance sampling. Though this does diminish quickly as the
dimensions of the search increase.
